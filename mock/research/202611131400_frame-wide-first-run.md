# Frame Wide at 65536 Records

This note records the `frame_wide` bench member at its boundary size, 65536 records, on 2026-11-13. The run exists to prove the machinery: a support crate holding the workload once, seven arms that are thin cdylibs over it, a driver that validates every arm against the reference and against each other before it times anything, and committed negative controls that show the validation refusing a wrong frame. The numbers are what that machinery printed. They are a first look, taken with deliberately small timing knobs, and nothing below decides anything.

The figures are from the second run of the member, at 15:13 and 15:17, after a review found three things that made the arms unfair to each other and one that made the parallel engine arm unreadable. Both chunked arms read the core budget once per frame inside the timed region and now read it once when the arm is built, and the collected chunked arm copied every chunk's output back on the calling thread after joining and now writes its own chunk on the thread that produced it. An arm whose timed region did not change moved between the two runs anyway: `hand` is within one percent under `heavy` and thirty-four percent faster under `light`, which is a measure of how far apart the two machine states were. No arm-by-arm comparison across the two runs is supportable, and only the within-run comparisons below are.

The engine arms in particular measure the engine as it stands. In op's words, 13 November: the engine arms measure something that "doesn't yet include any of the actual headline features like fusing and the adaptive trunk/fiber/morsel crunching on the datadlow-ish schedule. It's bare bones, barely lowers to anything interesting yet. So no calls should be made based on those yet, they are a baseline to compare against then." The intent there is that the engine numbers are a baseline for later comparison and carry no verdict; the rest is kept for provenance.

The frame is one simulation step over one input column: three independent pipelines of three stages each, a fan-in stage joining their three tails, and two independent single-stage transforms of the input. That is twelve work units over thirteen columns (the input, nine pipeline columns, the join and the two transforms). The plan in `202611131300_bench-matrix-for-the-intended-region.md` says ten columns for the same shape; the shape it describes needs thirteen, and the work unit count, twelve, is as planned. Two sweeps run it. `heavy` gives every stage eight rounds of a multiply, xor and shift mix, so the frame is bound by arithmetic. `light` gives every stage one wrapping multiply, so the frame is bound by memory traffic and by what each arm pays per stage.

## What was run

The member lives at `mock/benches/frame_wide/` and is one cargo workspace: the driver, `support/frame-wide-support`, and nine variant crates under `variants/` (seven arms and two negative controls). It is built and run from that directory:

```
./run-checks.sh
<target-dir>/release/frame_wide --n 65536
```

`run-checks.sh` is the entry point for everything that has to pass before a timing counts: the build, the grouping test, the support crate's unit tests and the two negative controls, in that order, silent on success. It exists because this member declares its own `[workspace]`, as every bench member here does, so it is not among `mock/Cargo.toml`'s members and `cargo mock test` never builds it. Before the script the tests passed because somebody had run them by hand, which is not a state anything can be checked in. `cargo mock bench list` shows the member as `frame_wide/heavy` and `frame_wide/light`, and `cargo mock bench run` cannot run it, because the launcher hands a member key to the repo's root driver, which knows only the root manifest (ticket `c9d2fc212037`).

Build profile: release, `opt-level = 3`, `lto = "fat"`, `codegen-units = 1`, no `target-cpu` flag, so baseline x86-64 code generation. Toolchain `nightly-2026-05-28`, `rustc 1.98.0-nightly (57d06900f)`. The harness revision this run resolved to is `a81e195`, which is written down here and nowhere else. It is not pinned and it is not recorded by the run: `Cargo.toml` takes the harness at `branch = "dev"`, `mock/benches/.gitignore` ignores `Cargo.lock`, and the meta files carry only the repo's own `git_commit`, which for this run reads `c1eee3f5-dirty` because the tree had the fixes in it uncommitted when the sweeps ran. So a rebuild from a fresh clone gets whatever `dev` is that day, and nothing in the committed artifacts would show that it differed. Host: four cores of an Intel Xeon at 2.1GHz with AVX2 present but unused by the build, 16GB of memory. No pinning (it does nothing off macOS) and no hardware counters, so every figure is a wall-clock median with a bootstrap interval. Timing: two passes of twenty runs, batches of five calls, one harness run, one zero-millisecond cooldown cohort, warm and cold modes, eight samples per arm per mode. The host was idle apart from the bench while it ran: nothing else was building, and a process listing during the run showed the bench and its workers and nothing else above a few percent of one core.

The seven arms are these. `engine` is the twelve work units on `Scheduler::run`. `engine_par` is the same scheduler on `Scheduler::run_parallel` over a `ThreadPoolApi` executor with four workers, spawned once and parked between frames. `hand` computes the whole frame per record in registers and writes only the three output columns. `hand_par` runs `hand` over one contiguous chunk of records per core. `rust_nostd` materialises every intermediate, one index loop per stage, into buffers allocated before the timed region. `rust_std` writes the same twelve passes as `map` and `collect` into fresh vectors every frame. `rust_std_par` runs `rust_std` over one chunk of records per core, each thread collecting its chunk's three columns into fresh vectors and then copying them into its own chunk of the outputs. That copy is a choice rather than a necessity, and it is made so the arm ends a frame in the same state `rust_std` does, with the three outputs each one allocation. A chunked form could instead keep every thread's vectors and hand the digest a list of chunks, which would drop the copy from the timed region, at the cost of the arm no longer being the chunked form of the arm beside it. What the arm no longer does is run that copy serially on the calling thread after every chunk has been joined. Both chunked arms spawn their threads with `std::thread::scope` inside every frame, so they pay a spawn and a join per frame that `engine_par` does not.

`run_fused` is not an arm. It needs a `FuseCarrier`, which folds a linear read-after-write sequence of `RecordOp` maps, and this frame is a graph with a fan-in, so there is nothing for it to fold.

## Correctness before timing

Every cell went through `validation::validate` before `harness::run`, called from the member's driver, with `may_differ = false` and `required = true` in both sections. The routine overrides `validate_output` to recompute the expected digest from the reference `record` alone, so each arm is held to the reference on its own as well as byte-exact to its neighbours. The digest is the FNV-1a hash of the join column and of the two transform columns; every pipeline feeds the join, so a stage dropped anywhere moves it.

Both cells passed: all seven arms agreed with the reference and with each other over four seeds of 65536 records each, and all seven were deterministic over the same four seeds. The driver also refuses a cell in which any arm was dropped before comparison, which the harness otherwise allows.

`tests/negative_control.rs` runs two wrong arms, one for each place the frame's topology is written down, at 4096 records and three seeds, in both sweeps. `drop_stage` is the fused path with the second stage of the first pipeline missing, which tests the statement of the frame in the `frame_record` switch. `engine_drop_stage` is the engine's own builder call sequence with the same `Map` omitted and `A3` taken from `A1` instead, which tests the other statement of the same shape and the one a reader can least check by eye: thirteen `Column` lines and twelve `Map` and `Join` lines whose correctness is in which type parameter sits in which position. A control over the first says nothing about the second, so each runs beside a good arm of its own kind, `hand` and `engine`. In all four runs the cell was refused, the refusal named the wrong arm, and it counted exactly six mismatches across three seeds: one per seed against the reference and one per seed against the good arm. A check refusing everything would have counted the good arm too, and a check doing nothing would have counted zero, so that one count carries both halves of each control.

The support crate's own tests hold `build_input` to `build_input_bytes` over five seeds in both regimes, tag and records. Only the byte image is reached by a run, so a drift in the by-value builder would show nowhere, and its doc comment asserting it is correct would be the only thing saying so. A second test checks that two seeds do not produce the same records, so two builders that both returned a zero column would not pass.

## Per-call cost and occupancy at 65536 records

The figures are the medians of the timed region only, which is the frame. Loading the input and computing the digest sit outside it for every arm; the findings files call that span the bridge, and it is about one millisecond a call at this size, nearly all of it the digest.

A median says how long a frame took and nothing about how much machine it took to get there, and for this bench that second number is the whole point of the parallel arms. The cores column is process-wide CPU, fields 14 and 15 of `/proc/<tgid>/stat`, over a fifteen second window on a worker running one arm's frame back to back, printed by `./occupancy.sh <arm>`. The figures below are medians of three such windows, and `./occupancy.sh all` writes all three readings per arm and regime to `mock/benches/frame_wide/occupancy.txt`, which is committed beside the bench. Read the readings rather than the median where the spread matters: it is a tick or two for every single-threaded arm and much wider for the parallel ones.

| Arm | heavy, ms | heavy, cores | light, µs | light, cores |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| `engine` | 2.99 | 0.99 | 173 | 0.99 |
| `engine_par` | 3.01 | 1.11 | 325 | 1.00 |
| `hand` | 3.39 | 0.99 | 71.3 | 0.99 |
| `hand_par` | 1.03 | 1.74 | 192 | 1.03 |
| `rust_nostd` | 2.84 | 0.99 | 190 | 0.99 |
| `rust_std` | 2.93 | 0.99 | 162 | 0.98 |
| `rust_std_par` | 1.34 | 2.22 | 524 | 1.64 |

Read the cores column for what it is. It covers the whole worker loop, the untimed bridge as well as the frame, and the bridge is single-threaded and about a millisecond a call, so every parallel arm's figure is pulled toward one. It is not pulled equally: the bridge is about half of `hand_par`'s window at a frame of 1.03 ms and about a quarter of `engine_par`'s at 3.01 ms, so the dilution is heavier where the frame is shorter. Each figure is therefore a floor on that arm's in-frame spread rather than a measure of it, and an arm reading higher than another did use more machine, while the ratio between two of them is not the ratio of their in-frame parallelism. The readings behind each figure are in `occupancy.txt` beside the bench, and a later reader should open those rather than trust the median alone. Per-thread sampling over `/proc/<tgid>/task/*/stat` is worse and does not fix it: an arm that spawns per frame has a different short-lived thread set at every sample and a reaped thread's ticks are gone, which on the run that first measured it put `hand_par` at 0.48 cores against this instrument's 2.16 on the same build.

The full reports, with intervals, are `mock/benches/frame_wide/heavy_n65536_findings.md` and `light_n65536_findings.md`, beside their CSVs and meta files. Every delta in them is taken against `hand` rather than against an engine arm, and each opens with the caveat above it, so a reader who finds one of those files alone and never reaches this note still meets op's words before any number.

Some of what the table shows is a property of the bars rather than of anything they are compared against, and is recorded so that nobody reads it as more. Under `heavy`, `hand`'s median is above all three column-at-a-time arms although it touches far less memory. The findings files give those gaps as 12.0% and 15.5%, both relative to `hand`, which is their baseline. Taken the other way round, against the arms themselves, the medians in the table above put `hand` 13.4% over `engine` and 19.4% over `rust_nostd`; the two directions differ because the denominator does, and the findings files are the ones to quote. Neither separation survives the multiplicity adjustment at eight samples (adjusted p 0.1055 for both), and the gap to `rust_std` does not clear the unadjusted test either (adjusted p 0.2891). Why the fused form does not win under `heavy` was not looked into. Every arm does emit the SSE2 packed multiply `pmuludq` somewhere in the support crate's own code in both regimes, which `./vectorisation.sh` writes out to `vectorisation.txt` beside the bench: per arm, per regime, the count and how many symbols carry it, with the instruction window around the first occurrence. The filter is the support crate's symbols rather than the frame body alone, so the artifact says the instruction is reached in the arm's own code and not that it is reached in the frame's inner loop. The counts differ by a factor of twenty across the arms, so the artifact says the instruction is reached and does not say the arms vectorise alike. Under `light`, both chunked arms are slower than their single-threaded forms, which at a frame of seventy microseconds is consistent with the per-frame thread spawn they pay. With eight samples an arm, the intervals are wide and the per-pass autocorrelation the reports flag is an artefact of two passes.

## Why `engine_par` runs on one core

`engine_par` is the one row that would be read wrong from the medians alone. Under `heavy` it sits at 3.01 ms against `engine` at 2.99, which reads as four cores buying nothing, and under `light` it is nearly twice the single-core arm. The cores column says the four cores were never there: 1.11 under `heavy` and 1.00 under `light`, against `hand_par`'s 1.74 over the same frame on the same budget.

The cause is in the engine rather than in the bench, and it is the waist-bounded phase axis. `mock/benches/frame_wide/tests/engine_grouping.rs` holds it as six assertions over the engine's own `grouping_arrays`, `phase_of`, `trunk_of` and `core_phase_mask`, called on the real `Map` and `Join` types the arm registers, so what follows is read out of the plan and not modelled.

The frame's read-after-write depths carry five, three, three and one unit: the five units reading the input, the three second stages, the three third stages, and the join. `waist_detect_const` flags a depth whose level width is a strict local minimum among the occupied depths on either side, and its scan runs over the interior of the occupied list only. Depth one is not a minimum, because depth two is no wider. Depth three, the join, is the narrowing this DAG actually has, and it is the last occupied depth, which the interior scan never reaches. So no waist is found, and `compute_phases_waist` gives all twelve units phase zero.

One phase is what does the damage. A trunk is a within-phase component over column conflict, and the join reads all three pipeline tails, so with everything in one phase the component closes over all ten units of the three pipelines and the join. `T1` and `T2` read only the input, which nothing writes, and each writes a column nothing reads, so each is a trunk of one. Three trunks, sizes ten, one and one. Trunk-to-core ownership is per-phase round-robin over the within-phase trunk rank, so core zero takes the trunk of ten, core one takes `T1`, core two takes `T2` and core three takes nothing.

The engine does have two paths that split a frame across cores by record range, and this frame reaches neither. `run_core_phase` takes the head and tail convergence split only for a phase carrying exactly one trunk, and this phase carries three. `worker_accum_unit_outer` gives every core its own record slice over the whole carrier, but the worker takes it only when the carrier is unit-outer, which the plan sets only when some unit writes an accumulator; this frame registers columns and nothing else.

One thing the account above does not explain. If core zero runs ten of twelve equal units while `T1` and `T2` run beside it, the frame's critical path should be about five sixths of the single-threaded one, so `engine_par` should come in around seventeen percent under `engine`. It does not: 3.01 ms against 2.99 under `heavy`, and worse than `engine` under `light`. So the grouping accounts for the missing three cores and not for the missing speedup of the one unit of work that was moved off the critical core. What eats it was not established. The candidates are the per-morsel dispatch walk, which every core pays over all 256 morsels whether it owns a trunk in the phase or not, the cost of waking and parking the pool each frame, and memory traffic shared with the two cores that do run. Separating those wants a profile rather than a wall clock, and it is not in this note.

Nothing the bench controls changes any of it. The record count does not: ownership is by trunk, and 65536 records at the default morsel size of 256 run 256 morsels, every one of them firing the same one core's trunks. The worker count does not: the busiest core holds twelve, eleven, ten, ten, ten, ten, ten and ten units at one through eight workers, which the grouping test asserts across that range. The executor is asked for four workers and is handed four closures, so nothing is lost between the request and the spawn.

Two candidate fixes are written up in ticket `2e38f730c0e4` and neither is taken here, because both are engine work and this is a bench. The first is the waist detector's interior scan, which means a DAG whose narrowing is its final stage gets no waist at all; whether that is deliberate, on the grounds that a trailing narrowing opens no following phase, or an off-by-one, is a question for the plan's design. With the join a waist this frame would be two phases: eleven units in phase zero whose five write-disjoint components are five trunks, and the join alone in phase one, which the head and tail path then splits across every core by record range. The second is the ownership policy, which `dispatch/core_mask.rs` already names as the simplest correct distribution with a load-balanced policy left as a later fork. A frame with one fat trunk and two thin ones is the case that fork is for.

## What a large cell costs

The large sweep (1048576, 4194304 and 16777216 records) is configured in `bench.toml` and was not run. On x86_64 every worker process first sleeps three seconds calibrating its counter (ticket `3ec38cb5c7c8`), and one cell at these knobs spawns 63 workers across the seven arms: five validation probes, one validation worker and four timing workers each, plus one sanity worker. That is about three minutes of fixed cost per cell before any frame runs. Each measured cell at 65536 took about three minutes and twenty seconds from start to finish, of which about ninety seconds were the timed phase, and nearly all of both phases was that sleep.

Scaling the measured frame cost linearly with the record count, which a frame that leaves the cache will not honour, and adding the untimed load and digest at the measured rate of about 15 ns a record, a `heavy` call at 16777216 records costs about one second and a `light` call about a third of a second. Each arm makes about 110 calls per cell between validation and timing. So one `heavy` cell at 16777216 records is about thirteen minutes of calls plus the three minutes of fixed cost, about a quarter of an hour, and a `light` one about eight minutes. At 1048576 records the fixed cost dominates and a cell is four or five minutes. These are extrapolations from one size, not measurements, and the first large run should replace them.
